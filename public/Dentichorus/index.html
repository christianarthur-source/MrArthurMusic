On stage like a Lion

Tongo

Polynesian folk song · arr. Greg Gilpin · 2-part

UpbeatBody percussionCall & response

Tongo is a canoe song from the Pacific Islands. Its exact origin is debated, and choirs sing many versions of the words. Picture paddlers pulling together on every beat, and sing it with that same teamwork.

Practice tips

  • Pat a steady beat on your legs while you listen. Never rush!
  • Snap your consonants: crisp T's and G's carry across the room.
  • When the parts split, sing yours while someone else sings theirs.

Calico Tree

“Under the Calico Tree” · Dick Thompson · 2-part

LyricalTwo-part harmonyStorytelling

A gentle, singable song where the two parts weave around each other. Your job is to make long, smooth lines and tell the story with your face and your words.

Practice tips

  • Sing on “loo” first to learn the melody shape, then add the words.
  • Connect the notes like a smooth ribbon (legato). Breathe only at the commas.
  • Match the tall, round vowels of the singers in the video.

In the Dark of December

Lon Beery · 2-part treble, piano, optional flute

Dorian modeHopefulWinter poem

The words are inspired by a poem by Oliver Herford, with the hopeful idea that even in the darkest part of December, spring is on its way. The music uses the Dorian mode, a scale that sounds a little mysterious and a little bright at the same time.

re · mi · fa · sol · la · ti · do · re

Practice tips

  • Find the sixth note of the scale (ti). That bright note is the Dorian “flavor.”
  • Sing the dark, quiet parts softly but with full breath support.
  • Let the hopeful lines grow brighter and louder.

How to practice with a video

  1. Just listenPlay it once with no singing. Notice the mood and where it gets loud or soft.
  2. Speak the wordsSay the words in rhythm. Get the tricky spots right before you add notes.
  3. Hum alongHum or sing “loo” to lock in the melody of your part.
  4. Sing itSing with the recording at full voice and good posture.
  5. Go soloMute it and try alone. Check back to see what you remembered.

Ten minutes a day beats one hour on Sunday. Short, regular practice is how the music sticks.

Ear training gym

Train Your Lion Ears

Great singers are great listeners. Turn up your sound and pick a game. Every game is in the key of C, so do = C.

Pitch Match

Hear a note, then sing it back on “loo.” Turn on the microphone and the meter shows if you are too low, too high, or right on.

Find it on the keys

No microphone? Play the note, then tap keys until you find the one that matches. Click any key to hear it.

Tips: Hum the note quietly first. If you are too low, lift your eyebrows and think “up and over.” If you are too high, relax your shoulders and let the note settle.

Solfège Detective

You will hear do first so you know home base. Then a mystery note plays. Which syllable is it?

The solfège ladder

Tap a rung to hear it. Notice that mi–fa and ti–do are half steps, the closest neighbors on the ladder.

Higher or Lower?

Two notes play. Is the second note higher, lower, or the same as the first? Show it with your hand as you listen: hand up for higher, hand down for lower.

Difficulty:
Correct: 0 Tries: 0

Why this matters

  • Knowing which way the melody moves is the first step to singing in tune.
  • “Tiny steps” are half steps, like mi to fa. They are the hardest to hear and the most important to sing carefully.
  • Try singing both notes back after each round, then check your answer.
  • Challenge: close your eyes and draw the melody in the air while the song recordings play.

Sing the Pattern

Hear do, then read the four notes and sing them with solfège. Press Check me to hear the answer.

Look for steps (line to space, the next note up or down) and skips (line to line or space to space).

Note Namer

What is this note? Answer with its letter name. Remember the lines, E G B D F (Every Good Bird Does Fly), and the spaces, F A C E.

Correct: 0 Tries: 0

Curwen Hand Signs

Each solfège syllable has a hand shape. Higher notes are signed higher in front of your body. Tap a card to hear the note, then make the sign while you sing it.

Healthy singing

Singer's Toolkit

Your voice is an instrument you carry everywhere. Here is how to play it well and keep it healthy.

𝄞

Posture

  • Feet flat, shoulder-width apart, knees loose (never locked).
  • Tall spine, like a string is pulling the top of your head up.
  • Shoulders down and relaxed, chest high.
  • Sitting? Scoot forward, feet flat, back away from the chair.
♬

Breath

  • Breathe low and silent, like smelling a flower.
  • Your belly and ribs expand. Your shoulders stay still.
  • Let the air out slowly and steadily as you sing.
  • Take your breath before the phrase, not in the middle of a word.
♫

Tone

  • Make space inside your mouth, like the start of a yawn.
  • Sing on the breath. Never shout or push.
  • Use your light “head voice” for high notes. Floating, not yelling.
  • Listen to your neighbors and blend into one sound.

Breath Builder

Breathe in for 4 counts, then let it out on a steady “sssss” like a slow tire leak. Can you make the hiss last the whole time?

4Ready

Keep your shoulders still while you breathe in. Put a hand on your belly and feel it move out.

Vowels & Diction

Choirs sound unified when everyone shapes the same five pure vowels. Keep them tall, with your jaw dropped and lips forward.

ahfather
ehbed
eesee
ohgo
oomoon
  • Hold the vowel, then add the consonant at the very end of the note.
  • Snap ending consonants together (T, D, K) so the audience understands every word.
  • On “ee,” keep a little “oo” shape in your lips so it stays round.

Warm-ups to try every day

Body wake-upStretch tall, roll your shoulders back, shake out your hands and feet. Loose bodies sing better.
Lip trillsBlow air through closed lips like a motorboat (“brrr”) while sliding up and down. Great for breath and range.
SirensSlide on “ooo” from your lowest note to your highest and back, like a fire truck. Keep it light and easy.
Sol-mi-do echoSing sol-mi-do with hand signs, then go up a step each time. Builds tuning and solfège.
Tongue twisters“Red leather, yellow leather” and “unique New York.” Start slow, then speed up with clean diction.
Five-note scaleSing do-re-mi-fa-sol-fa-mi-re-do on “mah, meh, mee, moh, moo.” Match vowels with your neighbors.

Keep your voice healthy

  • Drink water all day. Your vocal folds love it.
  • Don't scream at recess or games. Cheer with claps!
  • Hum instead of clearing your throat over and over.
  • If it hurts or you sound hoarse, rest your voice and tell Mr. Arthur.
  • Get good sleep, especially the week of the concert.

Growing voices

  • Voices change as you grow. Some notes may feel different this year, and that is normal.
  • If high notes start to crack, sing lightly and let Mr. Arthur know. He can help you find your best part.
  • Never strain to reach a note. Easy singing is healthy singing.
  • Every voice in the room matters to our sound.
